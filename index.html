Jim Weisser

I've spent most of my working life in Japan building companies, working on technology policy and serving on boards. These days I work every day on Nareji, which uses AI to identify key dates, parties and obligations in agreements. I continue as CTO and head of product at SignTime, a Japanese e-signature service for sending contracts by email or SMS and getting them signed. I'm also doing development again for the first time in 30 years.

How I got here

I'm from Fort Worth, Texas, and studied chemical engineering, with an environmental engineering specialization, at Rice University. I came to Japan in 1993 to teach English for a year or two. Instead, I ended up working in internet infrastructure, telecommunications and software.

I co-founded PBXL, which was acquired by Cisco (BroadSoft), founded SignTime, an e-signature service, in 2020, and now work on Nareji, which helps companies manage their agreements after signing. I also make angel investments.

The work I like

I particularly enjoy helping established businesses succeed in industries that aren't very digital, including Japanese construction and food companies. It's the inverse Y Combinator universe: the business is already real, but software has yet to do much of the work. Getting the technology to work matters. Getting people to buy it, use it and keep using it has usually been harder.

Beyond the companies

I started working on encryption policy in Japan in 1996. At the ACCJ, I helped prepare its internet economy white paper and push for a Japan-US dialogue, and served as a governor and vice president. I also serve on the board of HOPE International Japan and have completed director training for both for-profit and nonprofit boards.

Outside work, there's family, the NFL, UFC, wine, education reform and AI. I love making new mistakes. Some of the expensive ones have their own page.
