Nareji

Current work · AI agreement management

I’m working on Nareji to help companies act on the agreements they have already signed. A contract can tell you when to renew, how much notice to give and what each party has agreed to do. Someone still has to keep track of those obligations.

Nareji uses AI to identify dates, parties and obligations in agreements. It is designed to work across signing platforms, email attachments and scanned paper. The aim is to help people see what needs attention and take the required action before a deadline passes.

SignTime

Founded 2020 · Electronic signatures and contracts

Jonathan Siegel and I had discussed e-signatures in 2018. After PBXL, I wanted a reason to believe that customers were ready to buy. COVID provided that reason in 2020, when coming into the office just to stamp paperwork became a much more obvious problem.

My assessment is that COVID pulled two to three years of SaaS demand forward. For us, that meant raising money in 2020 and 2021 rather than a bit later. The subsequent slowdown across SaaS made it clear that the rate of adoption during the emergency would not continue indefinitely.

The other substantial lesson has been how much consulting is needed to get software adopted in Japan. High-touch sales and support should be part of the initial launch. Customers need help working out how the software will fit their business.

Even then, “I’ll buy it if you have this feature” is not a commitment. Building the feature does not necessarily produce a sale, because the stated objection may not be the real one.

Communified

After Cisco · Closed

Cisco was shutting down the old PBXL business, and I thought I could help those customers continue elsewhere. COVID made it impossible to meet people, and the business didn’t work out.

I deliberately closed Communified and migrated the customers to another provider who could take care of them. I believe strongly in making sure customers have a good experience with you, including when you stop running the business, because your reputation continues after the company closes.

PBXL, BroadSoft and Cisco

Founded 2006 · Acquired by BroadSoft in 2015

PBXL provided cloud-based business telephony. When we started in 2006, I thought we were two years late. Looking back, I think we were about six years early. I understood why people ought to want it much better than I understood how quickly they would adopt it. I also underestimated distribution. We used partners and consulting, but a lot of the business came from strong outbound sales.

In 2014, I went out to raise money for growth and received four separate inquiries about buying the company. I took that as the market telling me to sell. We were already using BroadSoft’s software. Its founders still ran the company, and it had experience buying founder-led businesses to enter new countries.

The acquisition gave BroadSoft a team on the ground in Japan. We closed deals with SoftBank and KDDI and were making progress with NTT Communications, alongside the existing overseas business. We still lost our first big bid, but won the second. Customers could see that BroadSoft was serious about Japan.

When Cisco acquired BroadSoft, I wanted to get back to startups. I also had a responsibility to the people who had come with me. I stayed to help them find good opportunities inside Cisco and left in 2019.

Weisser Consulting / Weisser Ideas

Early 2000s · Consulting

I had just left Enron, a very large company that went bankrupt. I figured the worst I could do was start a consulting business and have the same outcome, but without the fraud.

The work included telecommunications expense management, data centers, security and project management. Much of it involved finding errors in metered billing or services that customers were paying for without using. Personal outreach brought in the work.

The business did okay. Small-business consulting is variable, though, and revenue depends heavily on how much work you can personally deliver. Seeing the same communications problems at different customers helped lead to PBXL.

Exstra KK

Mid-2000 to January 2001 · CTO / board director

I started with a consulting engagement and a small angel investment, then drifted into being a co-founder, CTO and board member. We were helping Japanese companies move from analog business to e-business.

There were six founders, none with substantial sales experience. Only two of us had meaningful pre-sales experience. I overestimated what the others could sell and underestimated the difficulty of working with people I knew only marginally professionally. I was young enough to think “director” meant “super-important officer.”

I left in January 2001 after seeing dissension among senior management. The company failed later, probably in 2002 or 2003. I still keep the share certificate in a frame to remind me of the experience.
