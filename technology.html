JIM WEISSER / TECHNOLOGY

Engineering & development

From computer games and chemical engineering to networks, voice systems and learning to build again.

How I got here

I started as many people did, playing games on computers. Apple Trek, Lode Runner and Telegard, I'm looking at you. A friend and I bought magazines with programs printed in them and typed the listings in together. That wasn't pair programming; the term hadn't been invented yet.

It wasn't clear that I wanted to spend my working life with computers. Like my uncle and grandfather before me, I studied chemical engineering at Rice University, with a specialization in environmental engineering.

Learning to build again

I'm doing development again for the first time in 30 years. I have some Unix background and years of experience with networks and software businesses, but I'm relearning programming across languages and frameworks. I don't have formal training in algorithms, data structures or software engineering.

AI lets me build and test ideas faster. I still need to understand enough to judge what the tools produce. GitHub is where you can find my public work.

The internet years

I moved into computer consulting and internet infrastructure in Japan in the mid-1990s. At PSINet, I had nine different roles over about four years as the business grew. The work ranged from engineering and customer support to running parts of the business.

PSINet, sometimes written PsiNet, went through a few slogans, including “The Internet Starts Here” and “The Internet Super Carrier.” For a while, we even had our name on the Baltimore Ravens’ stadium. That ended when the company went bankrupt and the stadium’s name reverted in 2002.

Telephony made the gap between engineering and customer expectations particularly clear. A service could make technical sense and still be missing something customers considered essential, such as connecting a fax machine.
